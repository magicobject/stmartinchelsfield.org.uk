A church built in layers

The earliest parts of St Martin of Tours date from the 12th century, and the church is mentioned in the Textus Roffensis, dated 1122 — so it is almost a thousand years old. Like most medieval parish churches, it wasn't built all at once — each generation added or altered what it needed:

  • 12th century The earliest parts of the church building.
  • 13th century The tower and a chapel were added.
  • 15th century A porch was built.
  • 19th century A clergy vestry was added.
  • 2007 The Brass Crosby Room extension was completed.

The church is Grade II* listed, recognising its special architectural and historic interest.

The Brass Crosby Room

Rather than imitate the medieval building, the 2007 extension — designed by Clague Architects — was built as a clearly contemporary "north aisle," linked to the existing church at the west end of the nave and through the tower.

The design was scaled deliberately subordinate to the historic nave, borrowing its proportions and materials, while still reading unmistakably as a modern addition rather than a pastiche. It was developed with the Diocesan Advisory Committee and the Parochial Church Council before going through planning.

Today the Brass Crosby Room gives the church space it never had before — for meetings, groups and events alongside worship. It is one room, with a kitchen and toilets. It is named after Brass Crosby. See Groups & Activities for what meets there, or get in touch about booking it.

Inside the church

The earliest dateable parts of the church are in the Early English style, characterised by the triple lancet windows at the east end. The glass in them has been replaced many times. The current windows were designed by Moira Forsythe in the early 1950s, replacing the Victorian glass destroyed by the blast from a flying bomb during the 1939–45 war. She also made the small window of St John in St John's Chapel.

The tower and tower arch were added in the 13th century. The wooden screen at the base of the tower arch was originally across the chancel arch, before that arch was enlarged in 1857 to improve the connection between the clergy in the sanctuary and the people in the aisles. The current pews were installed in 1886, replacing earlier box pews.

St John's Chapel, on the south side of the church, dates from the 13th century. It contains the memorial to Peter Collett, alderman and merchant of London, who died in 1607.

The names of the recorded rectors of the church are inscribed inside it and go back to the early 14th century (see the list below). The church has a ring of eight bells and an active bell-ringing group.

Brass Crosby

The Brass Crosby Room is named after Brass Crosby, who was Lord of the Manor of Chelsfield and Lord Mayor of London, and a champion of the liberty of the press. In the late 1700s he was imprisoned in the Tower of London after demanding that the minutes of Parliament be published; public support led to his release. Tradition links him with the saying "as bold as brass".

A memorial to Brass Crosby is on the north wall of the aisle of the church, and a Blue Plaque is on a post to the left of the Church Drive entrance.

The parish of Chelsfield

Chelsfield stands on high ground to the east of Orpington, only 16 miles from London, and has always been popular with people from London who come to visit, walk and live. The name probably comes from Saxon times and means "a cold place".

The chief manor houses were Woodlands, Chelsfield House, Hewitts and Court Lodge, next to the church. As in most English villages, the squires and the Lord of the Manor set up the system of land use and provided work for local people. Chelsfield is still active in farming, and there has been an association with cricket since 1731; the Cricket Club still flourishes.

The railway came to the edge of the parish in 1868 (Chelsfield) and in 1876 (Halstead, for Knockholt, which became Knockholt in 1900). It helped to start the big development at New Chelsfield, and let local produce such as perishable strawberries reach London fast.

During the 1939–45 war many bombs fell in the parish, but only one caused real damage: a V1 Flying Bomb brought down on Lillys, which killed the owners and their dogs. Many of the headstones in the churchyard were flattened by the blast, and the stained glass in the three lancet windows was destroyed. In the 1920s the Orpington Bypass destroyed the original cricket field and fire station and left the church isolated from the village; as traffic increased it became more of a barrier. Efforts are in place to keep the strong bonds between the church and the village.

The parish is in the Deanery of Orpington, the Archdeaconry of Bromley and the Diocese of Rochester.

Well Hill Mission

Well Hill Mission Chapel was built in 1893 on land donated by the Hart-Dyke family of Lullingstone Castle. It was originally part of the Parish of Shoreham, and was one of three mission chapels built for the growing number of hop and fruit pickers. Boundary changes in the middle of the 20th century transferred it to the Parish of Chelsfield.

It is about a mile east of Chelsfield Village, at the end of a single-lane track leading off Well Hill Road. For service times, see Services and the Calendar.

Famous connections

Miss Read

Miss Read was the pen name of Dora Saint (née Shafe), MBE, the best-selling novelist of the Fairacre and Thrush Green books. She was born on 17 April 1913 and died on 1 April 2012. On 2 March 1921, aged seven, she moved from Hither Green, South London, to Chelsfield.

Her Fairacre books are based on her early experiences in the village, the Village School and the surrounding countryside. The first, Village School, was published in 1955, and her word portrait of Chelsfield, Time Remembered, gives a particularly interesting picture of the village in the 1920s, before the Orpington bypass split it in half. She walked a mile and a quarter along Church Road to the Village School.

She attended St Martin's with her parents, who sang in the choir; her father occasionally played the organ. The organ's hand pump is still there, and was put to use again in October 1987, when the hurricane left the church without power for three weeks.

Edith Nesbit

Another famous author, Edith Nesbit (married name Bland), lived on the borders of the parish. Born in August 1858, she lived for three years in her early teens close to the station then being built, known as "Halstead for Knockholt", at Halstead Hall. She saw the construction of the 597-yard tunnel from Chelsfield and the cuttings at Knockholt in the mid-1870s.

The miners worked in terrible conditions and several were killed; their deaths are recorded in our church registers. Edith, a founder member of the Fabian Society, was very concerned for them, and it is recorded that she used to walk to Chelsfield Station. These experiences inspired her classic children's book The Railway Children, written in 1906.

Our Benefice

St Martin's is part of the Benefice of Chelsfield with Green St Green and Pratts Bottom. The proposal for a joint Benefice was approved in November 2019, and from 1 June 2021 St Martin's became part of it, with Revd Susan Atkinson-Jones as its Rector. Our neighbours at St Mary's, Green Street Green have their own website: www.smasch.org.

St Martin's is listed on the Small Pilgrim Places Network, and is a corporate member of the Prayer Book Society.
